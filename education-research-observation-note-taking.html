Basic Observation and Note-Taking

A research paper on active observation, field notes, event tagging, and preserving investigative context.

Reporting and Documentation Investigation Science

Abstract

Observation is the investigator's primary instrument. Before a device is used, before a session begins, and before evidence is reviewed, the investigator must be able to notice, describe, and preserve what occurred.

This paper examines observation and note-taking as core investigative practices. It distinguishes observation from interpretation, outlines practical field-note standards, explains real-time event tagging, and shows how raw notes become a reviewable case file.

The Investigator's Primary Instrument

Investigators spend far more time observing than collecting dramatic evidence. Quiet watching, listening, waiting, and documenting are not wasted time. They are the core of the process.

Observation is active. It is not simply looking around a room. It is deliberately noticing conditions, sounds, smells, movement, airflow, lighting, temperature, structure, people, equipment, and context. Good observation gives later evidence review something to stand on.

A recorder can capture sound, a camera can capture light, and a meter can capture a reading. None of those tools can replace the investigator's responsibility to understand the environment and document what was happening around the measurement.

Observation Versus Interpretation

An observation describes what was perceived. An interpretation explains what the investigator thinks it means. The difference is essential.

"I heard three knocks from the northeast corner at 9:22 PM" is an observation. "A spirit knocked three times" is an interpretation. "The temperature reading was 62 degrees near the window" is an observation. "A paranormal cold spot appeared" is an interpretation.

Mixing observation and interpretation weakens the record. If the notes begin with a conclusion, later reviewers cannot easily separate what happened from what the investigator believed at the time. Strong notes preserve the observation first and allow interpretation to occur during analysis.

Developing Observation Skills

Observation improves with practice. Investigators should slow down, scan the whole environment, use multiple senses, and look twice before deciding what they saw or heard. First impressions can be useful, but they can also be wrong.

Visual observation includes light sources, reflections, shadows, windows, mirrors, objects, floor conditions, wall surfaces, ceiling features, furniture, screens, and anything that may influence later claims. Auditory observation includes constant sounds, intermittent sounds, direction, volume, rhythm, and possible mechanical or outdoor sources.

Smell and touch also matter. Musty odors, chemical smells, gas-like odors, moisture, air movement, vibration, warmth, and cold should all be documented when relevant. These observations may identify environmental or safety issues before the case is misinterpreted.

Taking Good Notes

Good notes include time, location, people present, equipment status, environmental conditions, actions taken, and exact observations. Every significant observation should be timestamped. Devices and team clocks should be synchronized before the investigation when possible.

Locations should be specific. "Living room" is useful, but "northeast corner of the living room, three feet from the fireplace" is better. If outdoors, use visible landmarks, maps, or GPS when appropriate.

Notes should identify who was present and what they were doing. Human movement, whispering, clothing noise, flashlight movement, device handling, and team reactions are common sources of contamination. Recording those details protects the evidence review.

Field Journals and Diagrams

A field journal is the primary documentation tool. Paper notebooks remain valuable because they do not rely on batteries and provide a direct record. A bound notebook with numbered pages and pen entries helps preserve integrity. Mistakes should be struck through with a single line rather than erased.

Digital notes can be useful, especially when they allow photographs, audio, and searchability. They should be backed up, and a paper backup should remain available. Voice notes can help in low light, but they should be transcribed after the investigation.

Sketches and floor plans are often more useful than paragraphs. Room diagrams can show doors, windows, furniture, vents, mirrors, equipment placement, witness location, and event direction. A simple accurate sketch can preserve context that words may miss.

Documenting Events

Events should be documented chronologically. The record should show arrival, walkthrough, baseline readings, equipment placement, interviews, sessions, event tags, known contamination, environmental changes, breaks, and departure.

Event numbering helps review. A unique event ID allows investigators to connect field notes, photos, audio, video, environmental readings, and later analysis. Without a clear reference system, a case can become a pile of interesting fragments that cannot be correlated.

Known contamination should be documented as carefully as unusual events. A cough, dropped flashlight, moving team member, furnace cycle, car passing outside, dog barking, radio interference, or door opened by an investigator may become important during review.

The Five-Line Field Note Format

A practical field note can be built around five lines: time, location, observation, context, and follow-up. This keeps the note short enough to use in the field but complete enough to review later.

For example: "10:42 PM. Second-floor hallway, observer near stair landing, sound seemed to come from east wall. Heard three knocks, evenly spaced, moderate volume. Present: Todd, Sarah, Mike; no one speaking; HVAC running; recorder A2 active; exterior wind light. Follow-up: check pipes/east wall, compare A2 timestamp, ask team to recreate footstep and wall-tap sounds."

This note does not prove anything. That is why it is useful. It preserves the event so it can be studied honestly.

Real-Time Event Tagging

Real-time event tagging marks an event as it happens so it can be located later in audio, video, environmental data, and written logs. A tag is a bookmark, not a conclusion.

A verbal tag might be: "Tag, 10:42 PM, three knocks heard near east wall." The tag should be calm and factual. Avoid excited conclusions such as "it answered us" or "that was a ghost." Written and digital tags should include the same event details plus location, people present, equipment status, and follow-up action.

Start and end sessions with clear tags. Known contamination should also be tagged. Good tagging protects the case from forgetting, exaggeration, and later storytelling.

From Field Notes to Case File

Field notes are the raw record. The case file is the organized record. Notes should be transcribed as soon as practical, ideally within 24 to 48 hours, while memory can still clarify abbreviations. The original notes should remain preserved.

Cleaned notes may correct spelling, expand shorthand, add headings, and organize a timeline, but they should not change meaning. If new information is added later, label it as an addendum.

A strong case file allows another investigator to reconstruct what happened, where it happened, when it happened, who was present, what conditions existed, what files relate to the event, what ordinary explanations were checked, and what remains unknown.

Observing Under Pressure

Observation becomes harder when something startling happens. Heart rate rises. Attention narrows. People speak over each other. Details vanish. This is normal, but investigators must train for it.

When pressure appears, return to the basics: what happened, when did it happen, where did it happen, who was present, and what conditions existed? Calm team language protects the record. Assigning roles can help one person lead, one log, one watch equipment, and one monitor environment.

Do not let a group decide too quickly. Ask each person to write what they perceived before discussion. Independent observations are stronger than agreement created by suggestion.

Conclusion

Good observation and note-taking separate investigation from storytelling. They preserve what happened before memory, excitement, fear, or group discussion reshapes it.

The investigator's job is not to make every moment dramatic. It is to create a reliable record that can be reviewed, questioned, correlated, and interpreted responsibly. Without documentation, experience fades into memory. With documentation, investigation becomes reviewable.
