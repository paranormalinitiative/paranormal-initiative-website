Basic Observation and Note-Taking

A documentation paper on preserving observations before interpretation changes them.

Reporting and Documentation Investigation Science

Abstract

This paper treats observation and note-taking as the investigator's first instruments. Clear notes keep the record anchored in what happened before interpretation begins.

Field Context

Field events are often brief, ambiguous, and emotionally charged. A sound may last two seconds; a witness may remember a detail after the fact; a team member may move without announcing it. Notes preserve the surrounding conditions that later review depends on.

Motivation and Bias

Memory changes quickly after an unusual event. Fear, excitement, group reaction, and expectation can reshape what people believe they observed. Notes written close to the event reduce that drift.

Applied Method

Use timestamps, location, people present, device status, environmental conditions, exact words, and possible sources. Write observations separately from interpretations. A simple event note should allow someone who was not present to reconstruct the moment.

Conclusion

The best notes are plain, timely, and reviewable. They do not need drama. They need enough detail to show what was observed, what was checked, and what remains unknown.
