Reporting and Documentation Ethics and Professional Standards

Abstract

This paper defines the case report as the permanent record of an investigation. A report should communicate what was done, what was found, what was explained, and what remains uncertain.

Field Context

Reports may be written for clients, teams, public archives, historical partners, or internal review. Each audience needs clear language, privacy protection, and enough context to understand the limits of the work.

Motivation and Bias

Reports often become more confident than the evidence allows. Teams may want to comfort a client, defend their effort, or make findings sound significant. That pressure can turn weak observations into strong claims.

Applied Method

Use a consistent structure: case summary, permission, location context, team roster, equipment, timeline, observations, media review, natural explanations checked, findings, limitations, and recommendations. Separate facts from interpretations throughout.

Conclusion

A professional report does not need to be sensational to matter. Its credibility comes from clarity, restraint, preservation of uncertainty, and respect for the people and places involved.
